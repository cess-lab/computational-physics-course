# PHY4605 Course Topic and Difficulty Blueprint

This document is the source of truth for the topics, subtopics, difficulty tiers, and 13-week execution sequence of PHY4605. Use it before creating or revising weekly slides, learning notes, MATLAB Live Scripts, practicals, diagnostics, assessments, or capstone materials.

The course uses **13 planned instructional weeks plus one empty buffer week**. The buffer protects the course against public holidays or cancelled sessions and must not contain a new planned core topic.

## Cohort calibration

Design the course for a nominal cohort of 35 second-year physics students with familiar analytic calculus, electromagnetism, and projectile-motion experience but little or no dependable MATLAB experience. Do not assume retained proficiency from MATLAB Onramp even though Onramp remains required.

The default teaching assumptions are:

- most students can differentiate or integrate a familiar physics expression;
- most students cannot yet translate a physics equation into computational steps;
- only uncertain small minorities can create/index arrays, modify loops, or produce a labelled plot;
- no student should be assumed to distinguish `*` from `.*`, interpret a MATLAB error, or translate a physics equation into computational steps without explicit teaching;
- pre-class preparation and spontaneous participation are normally minimal;
- lecture support is provided by one lecturer, with one additional demonstrator during practicals;
- disengagement through cognitive overload is a greater risk than insufficient exposure to advanced theory.

Recheck these assumptions with the Week 01 diagnostic for each new cohort. A stronger cohort may receive more stretch work, but core assessment difficulty must not be raised solely because a few students progress quickly.

The intended course outcomes are 90% pass, a median grade of B, 50% completing Core work without remediation, and approximately 10% demonstrating distinction-level independent modelling. These targets require broad exposure with deliberately narrow pass-level mastery; they do not justify reducing validation or physical interpretation.

## Course philosophy

Use **broad exposure with narrow assessed mastery**.

The course remains physics-first, but ordinary passing students are not expected to write complete MATLAB programs from a blank page. The normal path is:

`physical picture -> variables and units -> equation in plain language -> pseudocode -> commented scaffold -> output -> one validation check -> physical interpretation`

Do not introduce mathematical notation, a numerical method, MATLAB syntax, several validation concepts, and an unfamiliar physical model simultaneously. At least one of those layers must already be familiar before another is added.

## Cross-week material consistency contract

All weeks must read as one course, even when the physical example changes. Lecture slides, learning notes, and lecture demonstrations for the same week use the locked lecture model, vocabulary, variables, units, sign convention, validation language, and physics-first sequence. An AI-enabled practical may instead use a distinct but analogous supplied-model context to test transfer of the week's computational method; it must state its own assumptions, variables, units, and validation reference clearly and must not introduce unfamiliar theory as an unscaffolded demand. The lecture-slide specification is the visual production source of truth: canonical decks are 16:9 image-based PPTX files generated through the installed `codex-ppt` skill using its Teaching Courseware style, approval gates, built-in image backend, recorded slide state, English presenter notes, and parent-led QA. Illustrations, equations, arrays, plots, code, and callouts depicting the same model must agree in states, directions, sample count, colour meaning, and abstraction; an explicit mapping is required when the representations differ.

Exact equations, equation fragments, symbol-plus-unit expressions, MATLAB code, and numerical evidence must be supplied as strict source assets when image generation could alter their meaning. Every numerical plot remains a MATLAB-generated PNG with retained source/data and must not be approximated or redrawn by image generation. When a novice-level representation barrier warrants it, learning notes and MATLAB Live Scripts embed a purpose-built or approved reused ImageGen scientific illustration directly in the material, with local provenance and a concise caption; it must match the slide, equation, code, and physical model exactly. After sample approval, each remaining slide is generated by one subagent that inherits the coordinating main agent's exact model identifier and reasoning effort; an unverifiable mismatch blocks the slide. See `PHY4605_Lecture_Slide_Design_Spec.md` for the Codex-PPT gates and QA contract.

## Difficulty tiers

Classify every topic and subtopic using these tiers.

| Tier | Meaning | Normal student responsibility | Assessment use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Core** | Required for an ordinary pass | Explain, trace, complete, modify, apply, or interpret with normal scaffolding | May be assessed directly for pass-level marks |
| **Working exposure** | Important breadth without independent reproduction | Use supplied equations/code, recognise the method, and interpret output | May appear with the method and syntax supplied |
| **Stretch** | Distinction-level depth or optional lecturer extension | Work more independently, compare alternatives, or justify advanced evidence | Must not be required to obtain an ordinary pass |
| **Removed** | Outside the calibrated course | No student responsibility | Do not teach or assess as part of PHY4605 |

Code written from scratch is stretch evidence in lectures and AI-free assessments. Core implementation normally means completing or modifying a short, heavily commented scaffold and explaining what its lines represent physically and algorithmically. AI-enabled practicals are the explicit exception: students may use generative AI to write code, but their pass-level evidence remains the model, units, plan, reproducible output, validation, interpretation, and individual defence.

## AI-enabled practical contract

Each active practical consists of ten progressive micro-challenges across three or four analogous supplied-model contexts. The contexts should differ from the lecture model when that makes transfer visible, while the practical supplies the required equation or data, assumptions, variables, and units. Do not require a student to discover unfamiliar physics, mathematics, and MATLAB syntax simultaneously.

Students work in groups of two or three and may use any generative-AI tool to formulate, write, debug, or improve code. Every group records the tool used, what was requested, what was accepted/modified/rejected, and the independent checks performed; complete chat histories are not required. Code alone is never sufficient evidence. Every micro-challenge requires the relevant prediction, model/units, computational evidence, validation, and physical interpretation.

After all groups lock their submission, the lecturer draws a question slot and a student within each group. The selected student gives a short defence: explain the model, trace the relevant code/output, and answer a validation or parameter-change follow-up. Use a rotating hidden ledger so no student is selected again within their group until every member has been selected. Establish one completed defence entry for every member before any member receives a second entry; then average each student's completed entries. When a cohort has more groups than question slots, reuse a slot only with a distinct post-submission parameter or defect variant. The practical portfolio is 12% group evidence plus a 3% cumulative individual defence ledger.

## Exit capabilities

### Ordinary pass

A passing student should be able to:

1. identify the question, inputs, outputs, variables, and units in a familiar physics problem;
2. arrange supplied steps or write short guided pseudocode;
3. read and trace a short, commented MATLAB script;
4. complete or modify a small section of supplied code;
5. run the script and identify the output that answers the physical question;
6. recognise a physical, logical, array, or basic MATLAB defect;
7. perform at least one appropriate validation check; and
8. explain the main trend or result in simple physical language.

A student must demonstrate at least one valid check in individual assessed evidence. Polished code cannot compensate for the complete absence of validation.

### Cognitive-weight guide

Across course materials and assessments, target approximately 10% model and units, 20% algorithm and pseudocode, 30% MATLAB/code reading, 15% numerical-method theory, 10% validation, and 15% physical interpretation. Use this to prevent syntax recall or advanced theory from displacing scaffolded computational reasoning; it is not a required mark split for every individual activity.

### Distinction

A distinction student may additionally:

- translate less familiar physics into an algorithm with limited scaffolding;
- write a complete implementation from scratch;
- compare methods or stopping rules;
- design multiple independent validation checks;
- diagnose subtler numerical failure modes; and
- justify limitations and method choices independently.

## Execution and buffer policy

- Plan and prepare new teaching content only for `Week01` through `Week13`.
- Reserve `Week14` as the empty buffer/recovery week.
- If a scheduled session is lost, shift the remaining instructional sequence into the buffer rather than deleting a core learning outcome without review.
- If no session is lost, use the buffer only for optional revision, short remediation, capstone completion, deferred defence, or consultation.
- Do not place a new numerical method, new assessable chapter, compulsory practical, or planned high-stakes deadline in the buffer.
- Weekly package numbers represent the instructional sequence. Calendar dates and Classroom due dates may shift when the buffer is used.

## Detailed weekly subtopics

### Week 01 — Physics to arrays and plots

Use one familiar analytical model. The week repairs Onramp knowledge through physics rather than treating Onramp completion as proof of retained skill.

**Core**

- identify a physical question and the required output;
- distinguish variables, parameters, and units;
- locate and run sections in a MATLAB Live Script;
- assign scalar values using meaningful names and comments;
- create a short array with colon notation or `linspace`;
- read array length and retrieve an indexed value;
- distinguish scalar/matrix operators from element-wise `.*`, `./`, and `.^`;
- calculate a familiar analytical model across an array;
- produce one plot with axis labels, units, title, and readable line style;
- predict the trend before running and compare it with the plotted output.

**Working exposure**

- simple logical comparisons;
- a small table of selected values;
- naming conventions that make units visible.

**Stretch**

- compact vectorised alternatives or additional plot formatting.

### Week 02 — Pseudocode, loops, and debugging

Reuse a familiar physical model so the new cognitive load is algorithmic rather than mathematical.

**Core**

- decompose the task into input, calculation, output, and check;
- express an equation in plain-language steps;
- write or arrange short guided pseudocode;
- identify loop start, current value, update rule, next value, and stopping point;
- trace the first two iterations by hand;
- complete missing lines in a commented loop scaffold;
- modify one parameter and predict the effect;
- distinguish one syntax defect, one array/operator defect, and one physical/logical defect;
- use the MATLAB error location and surrounding line as diagnostic evidence;
- check one initial value, expected sign, bound, or known result;
- state what the graph means physically.

**Working exposure**

- preallocation;
- supplied helper functions;
- comparison between loop and vectorised forms.

**Stretch**

- write a reusable MATLAB function independently;
- design a second independent test.

### Week 03 — Two-by-two linear systems through Kirchhoff circuits

Keep the physical network small and use only two unknowns.

**Core**

- identify unknown node voltages or loop currents;
- choose and maintain a sign convention;
- derive two Kirchhoff equations from the circuit;
- map coefficients and source terms into a 2×2 matrix equation;
- preserve units in each row;
- solve using MATLAB's backslash operator with supplied syntax;
- reconstruct currents from the solution;
- validate through direct substitution or one node-current balance;
- interpret voltage ordering and current direction physically.

**Working exposure**

- alternative but equivalent equation ordering;
- the idea that some systems are poorly specified or sensitive.

**Stretch**

- algebraic/scaled residuals;
- rank and singularity;
- condition number;
- power-balance validation;
- diagnosing a plausible matrix that encodes the wrong circuit.

### Week 04 — Parameter sweeps and graph interpretation

Replace eigenproblems completely. Use an accessible analytical model such as projectile motion or a mass–spring oscillator so attention remains on computational experimentation and graph meaning.

**Core**

- state the baseline model, assumptions, and output;
- predict how one physical parameter should change the output;
- choose a small justified parameter range;
- hold all other inputs fixed;
- create the parameter array;
- complete a supplied loop or array evaluation for the sweep;
- plot several labelled curves with a legend;
- identify baseline, lower, and higher parameter cases;
- describe the trend using physical language rather than only visual description;
- identify an expected or limiting case as validation;
- record which parameter changed and which quantities remained fixed.

**Working exposure**

- a supplied two-parameter result table;
- normalised plots for comparing shape rather than magnitude.

**Stretch**

- heatmaps;
- quantitative local sensitivity;
- automated detection of a threshold or optimum.

**Removed**

- eigenvalues and eigenvectors;
- coupled-oscillator normal modes;
- generalised eigenproblems;
- mass orthogonality;
- eigen-equation residuals;
- symmetry breaking and eigenproblem limiting cases.

### Week 05 — Root finding

Use one physical equation with a visually understandable residual.

**Core**

- distinguish the original physical equation from its residual form `f(x)=0`;
- interpret positive, negative, and zero residual values;
- identify a root on a graph;
- identify a sign-changing bracket graphically and numerically;
- explain the bisection midpoint and interval update in plain language;
- trace and complete a heavily commented bisection scaffold;
- interpret Newton's tangent/update idea;
- trace and complete a heavily commented Newton scaffold;
- use one supplied stopping rule consistently;
- substitute the computed root into the residual as validation;
- explain the physical meaning and unit of the root.

**Working exposure**

- the difference between a guaranteed bracketed method and a faster local method;
- iteration count as a limited measure of computational cost.

**Stretch**

- uniqueness proof through monotonicity;
- comparison of several stopping criteria;
- MATLAB `fzero` as a reference solver;
- safeguarded Newton methods;
- formal solver-selection arguments.

**Removed**

- deliberately extreme small-derivative stress cases;
- a 150-radian Newton-step example or equivalent advanced failure construction.

### Week 06 — Numerical differentiation

Use a familiar position, field, or temperature relationship with a known derivative.

**Core**

- interpret the derivative as a physical rate or gradient;
- estimate a derivative from two nearby values;
- complete a forward-difference scaffold;
- calculate the units of the derivative;
- compare estimates for a small set of step sizes;
- compare with an analytic derivative or supplied reference;
- calculate or read a simple absolute/relative error;
- explain why a numerical derivative is an approximation.

**Working exposure**

- central difference using supplied code/formula;
- a supplied error-versus-step-size curve;
- the qualitative idea that extremely small steps may not always improve a result.

**Stretch**

- derivation of truncation-error order;
- detailed truncation-versus-round-off analysis;
- higher-order difference formulas.

### Week 07 — Numerical integration

Use an accumulated physical quantity such as work, charge, impulse, or distance.

**Core**

- connect area under a curve to a physical quantity and its units;
- identify integration limits and tabulated/sample values;
- interpret the trapezoidal rule geometrically;
- complete a scaffolded trapezoidal calculation;
- use MATLAB `trapz` with supplied syntax;
- compare two or three resolutions;
- compare with an analytic result or high-resolution reference;
- report the integral with correct units and a simple error statement.

**Working exposure**

- a supplied Simpson-rule result;
- convergence rate interpreted from a table.

**Stretch**

- derive quadrature error order;
- implement and compare several composite rules independently;
- adaptive quadrature.

### Week 08 — ODE simulation

Use one first-order physical system with a familiar differential equation and initial condition.

**Core**

- identify state variable, derivative, initial condition, timestep, and final time;
- translate the ODE into an Euler next-value rule;
- trace the first two updates;
- complete a commented Euler loop scaffold;
- produce and label the simulated trajectory;
- compare at least two timesteps;
- compare with a supplied exact solution or `ode45` reference;
- apply one appropriate bound, trend, initial-value, or conservation check;
- interpret the trajectory and state one model limitation.

**Working exposure**

- read a supplied `ode45` call and output;
- qualitative stability or conservation behaviour.

**Stretch**

- derive Runge–Kutta methods;
- determine a formal stability boundary;
- stiff systems, event handling, or coupled nonlinear systems.

### Week 09 — Data fitting and uncertainty

Use a small physical dataset with meaningful parameter units.

**Core**

- plot measured data with labelled axes and units;
- identify the proposed physical relationship;
- use a supplied linearised or direct fitting scaffold;
- extract and interpret fitted parameter values and units;
- overlay model predictions and data;
- calculate or inspect residuals;
- identify obvious residual structure or a poor model trend;
- state uncertainty cautiously using a supplied estimate or plausible range;
- distinguish a fitted parameter from a directly measured quantity.

**Working exposure**

- interpret a supplied fit-quality statistic;
- compare two plausible simple models;
- simple parameter uncertainty reported by MATLAB or supplied calculation.

**Stretch**

- weighted or nonlinear fitting;
- formal confidence intervals;
- covariance, regularisation, or advanced model-selection metrics.

### Week 10 — Random sampling and Monte Carlo simulation

Use a physically accessible random walk or repeated-estimation problem.

**Core**

- explain what one random trial represents physically;
- read and modify a supplied random-sampling scaffold;
- run repeated trials;
- display a trajectory, histogram, or distribution summary;
- calculate and interpret mean and spread;
- compare results for two or three sample sizes;
- explain why repeated runs differ;
- use a fixed random seed for reproducibility when required;
- report an uncertainty or variability statement.

**Working exposure**

- a supplied standard-error or convergence estimate;
- expected random-walk scaling interpreted graphically.

**Stretch**

- distribution derivations;
- statistical-mechanics simulations;
- variance-reduction methods or MCMC diagnostics.

### Week 11 — Sensitivity and uncertainty propagation

Build on the Week 04 parameter-sweep skill but now treat the input range as uncertainty evidence.

**Core**

- identify one uncertain input and its plausible range;
- choose a baseline, lower, and upper value;
- hold the model and other inputs fixed;
- complete a one-at-a-time sweep;
- report the resulting output range;
- identify which output is most affected;
- distinguish numerical approximation error, parameter uncertainty, and model limitation in plain language;
- state a practical conclusion without false precision.

**Working exposure**

- a supplied percentage sensitivity;
- a supplied first-order propagation formula;
- comparison of two uncertain inputs one at a time.

**Stretch**

- derivative-based propagation derived independently;
- Monte Carlo uncertainty propagation;
- interaction effects, global sensitivity, or correlated inputs.

### Week 12 — Integrated method selection and capstone studio

Use familiar cases and supplied evidence. Do not introduce a new required numerical method.

**Core**

- recognise which learned method matches a familiar problem type;
- identify the required input, output, and one key limitation;
- inspect a supplied or AI-assisted script for model, unit, array, or algorithm defects;
- trace the part of the script that implements the method;
- choose one validation check from a supplied menu and justify it;
- record accepted, modified, and rejected AI assistance;
- complete the capstone model, pseudocode, modification, output, validation, interpretation, and limitation evidence;
- rehearse an individual code-tracing and physics explanation.

**Working exposure**

- compare two feasible methods using supplied tables or plots;
- reproduce a result from a clean-session checklist.

**Stretch**

- transfer to an unfamiliar case with limited scaffolding;
- design a second independent validation check;
- implement an optional extension independently.

### Week 13 — Capstone presentation, defence, and handoff

The capstone is a bounded investigation, not an open-ended research project.

**Core**

- select from four to six lecturer-designed problem spaces;
- use a supplied starter Live Script;
- state the model, assumptions, variables, and units;
- complete or refine the pseudocode;
- modify one physical parameter or assumption;
- produce one principal graph or table;
- perform one required validation check and one chosen check;
- interpret the result and state one limitation;
- submit a concise reproducibility record and AI-use decision record;
- individually trace a relevant code section and answer a physics/validation question.

**Working exposure**

- respond to peer questions;
- reproduce the final output from a clean MATLAB session.

**Stretch**

- independently add a defensible extension;
- compare methods or validation strategies;
- investigate a less structured question.

### Week 14 — Buffer/recovery

No new topic is planned. If the buffer is unused, activities remain optional or restorative:

- revision through familiar code-tracing and graph-reading cases;
- short remediation for common diagnostic misconceptions;
- capstone completion or deferred individual defence;
- consultation and feedback;
- no new compulsory practical, method, or assessable chapter.

## Short remediation policy

When a weekly diagnostic exposes a common misconception, retain the planned schedule and publish an optional short remediation through Google Classroom.

Each remediation should:

- target one misconception only;
- take approximately 10–15 minutes;
- contain one plain-language explanation;
- show one worked correction using a familiar physical model;
- include one short retry question or code-tracing task;
- show the correct reasoning after the retry;
- end with one confidence check;
- link to the relevant approved note, Live Script section, or NotebookLM source;
- avoid becoming another long reading or a prerequisite for understanding the next lecture.

Track completion and common responses for teaching feedback, but keep the remediation ungraded unless the lecturer explicitly changes the policy.

## Weekly lecture-package difficulty contract

Every standard weekly lecture package should use:

- 12–14 core teaching slides plus no more than four optional appendix/stretch slides;
- no more than three genuinely new core ideas;
- one recurring physical model for the main explanation;
- a physical picture or question before dense notation;
- every essential equation restated in plain language;
- pseudocode before MATLAB implementation;
- short, commented MATLAB fragments rather than full technical listings;
- one prediction checkpoint;
- one code-tracing or modification checkpoint;
- one graph-interpretation checkpoint;
- one core validation check developed clearly;
- advanced derivations, secondary checks, and method comparisons in appendix/stretch material.

The lecturer may omit a planned stretch subtopic to make room for prediction, tracing, discussion, or interpretation. Coverage of stretch material is not evidence that core learning occurred.

## Assessment alignment

Across the course, target the following competency balance:

| Competency | Share |
| --- | ---: |
| Model and units | 10% |
| Algorithm and pseudocode | 20% |
| MATLAB and code reading | 30% |
| Numerical-method theory | 15% |
| Validation | 10% |
| Physical interpretation | 15% |

Use familiar physics with a changed parameter, supplied graph, short code defect, or new interpretation question for ordinary AI-free pass-level assessment. Fully unseen formulation, blank-page coding, and independent advanced method comparison belong to stretch/distinction evidence in that setting. AI-enabled practicals follow the separate practical contract above.

## Topic-review checklist

Before approving any weekly plan, ask:

1. Are there no more than three new core ideas?
2. Is the physical model familiar enough that students can focus on the computational idea?
3. Is essential mathematics introduced before it is used?
4. Is every essential equation translated into plain language?
5. Does pseudocode appear before code?
6. Can a passing student complete the task by tracing, completing, or modifying a scaffold rather than writing from scratch?
7. Is there exactly one clearly developed core validation check before additional checks appear?
8. Are working-exposure and stretch subtopics visibly separated from core requirements?
9. Can the core lesson be completed without pre-class preparation?
10. Does the week preserve time for prediction, code tracing, graph interpretation, and explanation?

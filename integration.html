Area becomes impulse

The area under the force–time curve is impulse. The trapezoidal rule uses a straight line between two samples. Simpson’s rule uses a quadratic through three equally spaced samples. Adding the panels estimates the total impulse.

Force is measured in N and time in s, so impulse is in N s, equivalent to kg m/s.

Check the result

The analytic reference is J = 12(0.8)[1 − exp(−2/0.8)] N s. The positive impulse must be smaller than the 24 N s rectangle formed by the initial force and the full duration.

Try 4, 8 and 16 intervals. Both methods use the same sampled force values. For this smooth example, both approach the reference as the intervals become narrower.

Where do Simpson’s weights come from?

Take three equally spaced samples: F₀ at t₀, F₁ at t₀ + h, and F₂ at t₀ + 2h. Fit one quadratic through them. The panel spans two intervals, each of width h.

  1. Centre the coordinate. Let u = (t − t₁)/h, where t₁ = t₀ + h. The samples now lie at u = −1, 0 and 1. Write the fitted curve as p(u) = Au² + Bu + C.
  2. Fit the samples. At u = 0, C = F₁. The two endpoints give A = (F₀ − 2F₁ + F₂)/2 and B = (F₂ − F₀)/2.
  3. Integrate the quadratic. Since dt = h du, integrate h(Au² + Bu + C) from −1 to 1. The odd Bu term cancels, giving h(2A/3 + 2C).
  4. Substitute A and C. The area becomes h(F₀ + 4F₁ + F₂)/3. That is Simpson’s 1/3 rule: the middle sample receives four times the endpoint weight.

For several panels, adjacent panels share endpoints. Their combined weights are 1, 4, 2, 4, …, 2, 4, 1. Composite Simpson’s 1/3 rule requires equally spaced samples and an even number of intervals. A quadratic is an approximation to this exponential force curve, so validate its total against the reference.

Numerical Integration: Accumulate a changing quantity
and test convergence
Estimate a physically accumulated quantity from sampled values, then test convergence: do refined
estimates approach a stable reference?

   Physical question. A force changes continuously with time and gradually decays. How can
   sampled force values estimate the total impulse delivered from t = 0 to t = 2.0 s?



Learning outcomes
1. connect area under a physical curve to an accumulated quantity, limits, and units;
2. trace a trapezoidal estimate and the equivalent MATLAB trapz(x,y) calculation; and
3. validate a numerical integral using refinement, an analytic/reference value, and simple error.


How to use this note
Read the physical question and model first. At each prediction checkpoint, pause and write what you
expect; then trace the short calculation, run the MATLAB listing, and compare the result with the
stated validation evidence.


1. Integration means physical accumulation
In calculus, an integral is often introduced as area under a curve. In computational physics, that area
usually represents a physical quantity. For a force that varies with time,
                                                  ∫ tb
                                             J=        F (t) dt
                                                     ta

is the impulse delivered over the stated interval.
The axes carry the unit of the integral. Force is measured in newtons and time in seconds, so

                                              N × s = N s,

which is also equivalent to the momentum unit kg m s−1 . The limits ta and tb are part of the physical
question: changing them changes what has been accumulated.

   Prediction checkpoint. If the force remains positive from 0 to 2.0 s, the impulse must be
   positive. If the force starts at 12 N and then decays, the impulse must also be smaller than the
   rectangle 12 N × 2 s = 24 N s.

3. A numerical integral only sees samples
A computer does not need the entire continuous curve to apply a basic quadrature rule. In plain language,
a quadrature rule estimates accumulated area by combining function values at selected coordinates. Here,
convergence means that, with the force model and limits held fixed, the numerical estimates approach
a stable value as the sample spacing is reduced. With four equal intervals between 0 and 2.0 s,

5. Add the trapezoids for a composite estimate
For uniform spacing, the four trapezoids can be collected into the composite form
                                          [                           ]
                                            1                      1
                               Jtrap = ∆t F0 + F1 + F2 + F3 + F4 .
                                            2                      2

Using the five sampled values above,

                                       Jtrap ≈ 9.0969821492 N s.

This result is positive and below 24 N s, so it passes the basic sign and bound checks. Those checks are
useful, but they do not yet tell us whether the approximation is accurate enough.


6. Pseudocode before MATLAB
 1. state the accumulated quantity, integration limits, and expected unit;
 2. choose a supplied number of intervals;
 3. create sample coordinates between the fixed limits;
 4. evaluate the force at those coordinates;
 5. add trapezoid areas between neighbouring samples;
 6. repeat at finer supplied resolutions without changing the force model;
 7. compare the estimates with an independent reference; and
 8. report the final integral with its unit, error, and physical meaning.

  7. MATLAB trapz performs the same accumulation
  The coarse calculation can be written explicitly and then checked against MATLAB’s built-in trapezoidal
  integration function:
                 Listing 1: Manual and built-in trapezoidal integration of the sampled force.
 1 F0_N = 12;
 2 tau_s = 0.8;
 3 T_s = 2.0;
 4 n = 4;
 5 t_s = linspace(0,T_s,n+1);
 6 F_N = F0_N*exp(-t_s/tau_s);
 7 dt_s = T_s/n;
 8 J_manual_Ns = dt_s*(0.5*F_N(1) + ...
 9     sum(F_N(2:end-1)) + 0.5*F_N(end));
10 J_trapz_Ns = trapz(t_s,F_N);


  The call trapz(t_s,F_N) matters: the first argument supplies the physical coordinates and therefore
  the actual spacing between samples. The manual and trapz estimates should agree for the same data.

      Runnable does not mean physically correct. The call trapz(F_N) runs, but MATLAB then
      assumes unit spacing between samples. If the real sample spacing is 0.5 s, reporting that raw result
      directly as an impulse silently loses the physical time scale.

10. Interpret the number physically
An impulse of about 8.83 N s corresponds to a momentum change of about 8.83 kg m s−1 in the positive
force direction over 0 to 2.0 s. This interpretation is conditional on the stated force model and limits;
numerical precision cannot repair an inappropriate model.


Guided extension: Simpson comparison and convergence evidence
Read and interpret the supplied eight-interval Simpson result, JSimpson = 8.8124455163 N s, as com-
parison evidence; independent derivation or implementation of Simpson’s rule is not required. In the
trapezoidal table, halving ∆t reduces error by about a factor of four; describe that observed convergence
pattern without deriving its formal order.


Optional extension
Optional extensions are quadrature error-order derivation, independent composite-rule comparison, and
adaptive quadrature. They are not required for this week’s pathway.


Three takeaways
1. A numerical integral represents physical accumulation over explicit limits, and its unit combines the
   two axis units.
2. The trapezoidal rule sums areas between neighbouring samples; trapz(x,y) uses their supplied
   coordinates.
3. Refine the sampling and compare with a reference before trusting the accumulated result.

   Preparation for the practical. Transfer the same reasoning to impulse, work, and charge. For
   each context, identify the accumulated quantity, limits, axis units, trapezoidal sampling/refinement
   plan, and validation reference.
